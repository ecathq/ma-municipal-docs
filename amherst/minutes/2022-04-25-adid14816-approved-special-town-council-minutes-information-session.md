2. Information Session: Open Meeting Law, Conflict of Interest Law, and Public Records
Law – KP Law Attorney Lauren Goldberg
KP Law attorney Lauren Goldberg spoke about questions posed by Councilors in advance of and during
the meeting, including the following topics:
• Open Meeting Law, including:
Memo or information shared by Councilors outside of public meetings, including those
o
expressing Councilors’ opinions, which cannot be shared until the document is made
public
A memo expressing a Councilor’s opinion on a topic could not be deliberated on until an
o
open meeting of the Town Council
Email is not to be used to express Councilors’ opinions outside meetings; discussions
o
concerning opinions should be at meetings
Best practice is to request to be heard by asking the Council President to place an item on
o
the agenda

---

Posting memos expressing Councilors’ opinions can create a situation where people are
o
inclined to respond to each other and the group
While the Attorney General has allowed an expression of one member’s opinion to be
o
posted publicly outside a meeting, it is inconsistent with the goal of the Open Meeting
Law to provide for deliberation to occur at a meeting
Schoen asked if a memo is written that expresses a Councilor’s opinion and is not shared before the
meeting, if the Councilor can use their three minutes during discussion to speak about the memo and ask
that it be added to the Council’s meeting packet.
Goldberg responded that a document becomes part of the record and listed in the minutes when it is
specifically referred to, used, and present at a meeting. What is listed in the minutes is what is discussed
at the meeting. Any time a Councilor shares their opinion outside of a meeting with a quorum creates a
risk of a violation, and it is worse when it is several days ahead. Use of an agenda item to present a memo
is a better way. Any kind of hearing where the Council will make a decision, it would be detrimental for
members to talk about their opinions prior to voting on the matter. As the Town’s attorneys, KP are able
to defend certain actions taken that may or may not constitute a violation of the Open Meeting Law, but it
is better not to go down that road at all.
Pam asked if Councilors can express their opinions at meetings or in newsletters or with one or two
Councilors. Pam stated that debate during meeting is limited due to full agendas, and that she feels that
this is not real.
Goldberg responded that Councilors can speak with state representatives to change the Open Meeting
Law. Goldberg spoke about the enforcement of the Open Meeting Law and stated that Councilors cannot
exchange emails among a quorum without violating the Open Meeting Law; a violation can occur in a
sequence. Email should not be used by Councilors to conduct Council business.
Goldberg stated that one way to vet issues is through a subcommittee, which is any two people authorized
to work together on a matter. Subcommittees are subject to all Open Meeting Law requirements.
Bahl-Milne asked about less than a quorum of Councilors research a topic and ask that it be placed on the
agenda and in the packet.
Goldberg responded that an email or gathering at one time or serially on matters that might come before
the Council are a violation of the Open Meeting Law.
Bahl-Milne asked if two Councilors can work on something outside a meeting.
Goldberg stated that two Councilors talking about something are not a violation, but if they are a
subcommittee or a quorum of a subcommittee it would be a violation.
Goldberg said it is very easy to wind up with a serial violation when there are a larger number of
members, and the more difficult it becomes to avoid Open Meeting Law violations.
Goldberg stated that a conversation between two members does not create a record, but emails that are
sent and received are public records, and records requests are common.
Rooney asked for clarification on discussion of an agenda item.
Goldberg responded that the discussion must be at an open meeting. Within a meeting, the Open Meeting
Law does not address the content of the discussion other than to say that the agenda has to include all the

Taub asked if Councilors can share their opinions and positions on issues with constituents and at district
meetings.
Goldberg responded that the Open Meeting Law does not regulate discussions with constituents or doing
individual research. Goldberg stated that if a Councilor was speaking at a district meeting and saw that a
quorum was present, the Councilor could not ask the other Councilors present if they agree or to share
their opinions on the issue being discussed.
Schoen asked if a Councilor were to write a 14-page paper on a topic on the agenda, has not shared it with
any other Councilors or made it public before the meeting, and has spoken to it during the meeting, if the
paper can become part of the packet.
Goldberg stated that it would be a public record if it is discussed in substance and is present at the public
meeting.
• Conflict of Interest Law, including:
Prohibitions on participating in a deliberation on an item on which a Councilor has a
o
financial interest or an abutter to an abutter within 300 feet
Perception of a financial interest, and filing of a disclosure

o

Pam stated she is a member of the Amherst Women’s Club and there was a vote at a prior meeting, she
has never been on the board or had a financial interest; the fact that a Councilor is a member should not
require that a Councilor recuse themselves.
Goldberg responded that if a Councilor were a member, not a member of the board, treasurer or had any
financial interest, then just being a member creates the appearance of a conflict of interest and the
Councilor would want to disclose that relationship; it is better to be safe than sorry. Conflict of Interest
violations are subject to criminal penalties, and the violation belongs to the member rather than the board.
Devlin Gauthier asked how often a perception of conflict of interest should be filed.
Goldberg responded that it depends on the situation; the perception may be different in different
situations. It is not okay to do a blanket disclosure.
Devlin Gauthier asked if a Councilor had a family member who was not financially tied to the Councilor
but had a financial interest in one of the items within a full budget would the Councilor need to recuse
themselves from the entire budget process.
Goldberg responded that if a family member has a financial interest, then the Councilor would have a
financial interest, however the budget review process is department by department, and the Councilor
could recuse themselves from the part of the budget process or article to be voted where the family

Griesemer asked about recusal.
Goldberg responded that recusal is non-participation, not sitting at the table, not abstaining; a Councilor
may decide to stay in the room or not but are not required to leave the room. To ensure that a recused
Councilor is not participating, they would leave the table and sit in the audience. The Councilor does not
have to disclose the reason they are recusing themselves; they can just say they are not going to
participate in this portion of the meeting.
Bahl-Milne asked if a matter could potentially increase or decrease the value of a Councilor’s home, if
that would be considered a financial interest.
Goldberg responded that an abutter, an abutter to an abutter within 350 feet, directly across the street or
diagonally across the street, then you cannot participate because it is presumed that you would have a
financial interest. To dispel the presumption, an appraiser could make an appraisal based on what is
happening.
• Protocol for a Councilor emailing a committee
• Councilors sharing on social media, newsletters, and webpages as spaces for sharing information
rather than opinions; it is not prohibited to send a newsletter with a Councilor’s opinion, unless
the opinion is regarding a particular matter that is coming before the Council and the Council will
adjudicate someone’s rights
• Not sharing opinions in public places outside an open meeting on topics when someone’s rights
will be adjudicated by the public body
Taub asked if Councilors can express their opinions in newsletters.
Goldberg responded that it is not prohibited for Councilors to send newsletters with their opinions in it,
unless they are writing about a matter before the Council where they will be adjudicating someone’s
rights or an entity’s rights.
Goldberg spoke about risks associated with Councilors sharing opinions on a specific matter before a
vote.
Griesemer spoke about any additional questions Councilors have that may be sent to the Town Manager,
and thanked Goldberg for her time.
