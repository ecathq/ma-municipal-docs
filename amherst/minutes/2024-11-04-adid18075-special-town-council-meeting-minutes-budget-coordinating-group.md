2. Presentations and Discussion
a. Financial Indicators
1) Presentation
Bockelman acknowledged and thanked Finance Director Melissa Zawadzki, as well as
Comptroller Holly Bowser, Treasurer/Collector Jennifer LaFountain, and Principal Assessor
Kim Mew.
Staff spoke about the following:
 Funding operating budgets responsibly and fairly
 Managing increases in health insurance and other fixed costs
 Building good working relationships among stakeholders
 Building long-term fiscal sustainability
 Prioritizing support for major capital projects
 Recognizing pressure on taxpayers
 Reaffirming the Town’s AA+ bond rating
 Continued strong financial position
 Approved a balanced FY2025 budget
 Successfully secured grants
 Excellent fiscal management and forward-looking planning
 Strong financial systems
 A team approach to solving problems
 Managed, steady growth
 A comparison of sources of revenue in FY2016 and FY2025 – in 2025, property taxes
make up slightly more of the total revenue than they did in 2016, whereas state aid and
local receipts made up slightly less. Revenues increased within the 3-4% forecasted range
between FY2025 and FY2016.
 A comparison of the Town’s expenditures in the FY2016 budget and FY2025 budget –
the percentages budgeted for the elementary school, Regional School District, and Town
decreased slightly between 2016 and 2025, while the percentages budgeted for capital,
miscellaneous, and unappropriated uses increased slightly. The percent budgeted for the
Jones Library stayed the same.

---

 Between those years, revenue increases in actual dollars have outpaced those in constant
dollars. This reveals that property tax revenue increased are not quite keeping pace with
inflation.
 Uncollected taxes as a percentage of the net property tax levy were highest in 2020 at
2.2% due to the COVID-19 pandemic. The percentage decreased to 1.44% in 2024; this
is 0.14% higher than the percentage in 2015. The 2024 rate of 1.44% indicates that the
Town’s recent collection rates remain favorable to bond rating agencies.
 Although state aid rates are increasing in comparison to inflation, state aid in constant
dollars has decreased since 2020.
 State aid as a percentage of operating revenues dipped in 2023 because during that year,
the Town made transfers into the general fund from the stabilization fund in the amount
of $5 million.
 State aid amounts continue to increase each year but so do state assessments. Net receipts
are slightly up but assessments are rising faster than state aid increases. The biggest
drivers of this trend are regional transportation and school choice and charter school
tuition.
 The primary sources driving revenue growth are building permits, motor vehicle excise,
meals tax, and hotel/motel tax.
 Economic growth revenues as a percentage of operating revenues between 2015 and 2024
dipped lowest in 2021 due to the COVID-19 pandemic but have since recovered to pre-
pandemic levels.
 There as significant growth in new building permits in 2024; these included three very
large apartment complexes.
 Economic growth revenues as a percentage of operating revenues appeared to drop in
2023 because the $5 million transfer from the stabilization fund artificially increased the
Town’s budget.
 Property taxes have increased from year to year but revenue from property taxes has not
increased in pace with inflation. As property taxes increase, so does the difference
between property tax revenue in real dollars and in constant dollars.
 As tax revenue sources, local receipts and state aid have remained relatively flat and have
kept pace with inflation more that property tax revenue.
 When adjusted for inflation, operating expenditures have actually decreased since 2020.
 Amherst’s low operating expenditures are both an indicator of financial efficiency and
evidence of certain challenges. Operating expenditures remain low due largely to the fact
that Amherst has many student residents who use Town services but do not pay property
taxes.
 Amherst’s operating expenditures per capita are lower than that of many of its peer
communities and then the statewide average.

---

 Between 2015 and 2024, the Town has added approximately 17.5 full time equivalent
municipal employees. The salaries of those employees made up a smaller percentage of
the total budget in 2024 than they did in 2015; this trend began between 2021 and 2022.
 Amherst’s debt expense in 2024 was quite low at 0.8%-- this percentage is down from
2.5% in 2015. This indicates a greater flexibility to issue new debts.
 The debt service percentage will rise in the next few years due to debt authorizations for
the new building projects.
 Amherst’s debt expense is significantly lower than that of its peer communities and the
statewide average. The Town maintains a strong AA+ credit rating.
 Outstanding debt as a percentage of assessed valuation decreased steadily from 2015 to
2023 but then increased between 2023 and 2024. Despite this, the 2024 percentage of
0.66% is still lower than the 2015 percentage of 0.86%.
 Hampshire County retirement is currently funded at just over 75% and is on track to be
fully funded by FY2032. The OPEB (other post-employment benefits) funded is currently
funded at just over 13%. Once retirement is fully funded, additional contributions will be
redirected to the OPEB fund.
 The Town has a financial policy in place that maintains free cash as 5% of the budget.
Another 10% is generally added once the budget is certified, bringing reserves to around
15% of the total budget.
 The Town is currently above its goal of 15% in reserves due to efforts to increase the
capital stabilization fund ahead of the four major upcoming capital projects.
 The dip in the stabilization fund between 2022 and 2023 occurred because $5 million was
taken out of the capital stabilization fund and put toward the ongoing school building
project.
 Reserves make up a greater percentage of Amherst’s operating budget compared to its
peer communities and the statewide average.
Bockelman presented on the status of FY2025, emphasizing the following points:
 Amherst was given a strong bond rating by S&P Global Ratings, which is the “gold
standard” for cities and towns.
 Amherst maintains healthy financial reserves (made up of free cash and stabilization
funds) of $28.6 million, or 29.4% of the operating budget.
 Budget highlights for FY2025 include good collaboration between Town staff and
elected officials, a 4% increase for operating budgets, and continued investment in capital
and sustainability.
 Ongoing financial challenges for FY2025 include inflation, regional assessment, the high
cost of health insurance, retirement and OPEB liabilities, and growing the tax base to
support the demand for new services.
Bockelman presented on the financial outlook for FY2026, emphasizing the following points:

---

 In FY2026, there are working assumptions that property tax will increase by 2.5%, new
growth will amount to approximately $650,000, state aid will likely remain flat or dip due
to potential economic downturn, local receipts will see moderate growth from the
previous year, and there will be no override.
 There is a working assumption that the budgets for the Town, elementary school, Region,
and library will all increase by 3% FY2026.
 For FY2026, two collective bargaining agreements with school unions are settled and
three are unsettled; for the Town unions, six collective bargaining agreements are
unsettled and none are settled.
 For the FY2026 budget, health insurance is projected to increase by 13%.
 Some of the major challenges for FY2026 include:
o For schools, the exhaustion of ESSER funds, the transition of the sixth grade to
the middle school, and significant increases in health insurance and transportation
costs
o For the library, Diversity, Equity, and Inclusion (DEI) efforts, patron demand for
services, growing dependence on endowments and fundraising, and staff
development
Finally, Bockelman emphasized the following points about the budget “big picture” going
forward:
 Most of the Town’s growth will come from its own resources
 Utilizing reserves, planning for sustainable development, and seeking additional funding
sources can help the Town manage its financial concerns
2) Discussion
The Town Councilors, school committee members, and library trustees discussed the following
points with the presenters:
 Including percent changes from year to year in future presentations
 Why projected budget increases (3% for Town, schools, and library) do not match the
projected revenue increase of 4.6%
 Increasing health insurance costs and self-insurance as a potential way to address this
challenge
 The work of the Health Insurance Advisory Committee, which includes members from
all the bargaining units in the Town and schools and explores ways to improve health
insurance management
 PILOTs (payment in lieu of taxes) as part of the budget management strategy—these
include agreements with UMass, Amherst College, and Hampshire College

---

 The benefits and drawbacks of conservative approach to local receipts in anticipation of
potential economic downturns, especially when investment income was so much higher
in FY2024 than in FY2023 and recurring yearly surpluses in the millions of dollars
 How to allocate budgetary surpluses to benefit a wider variety of Town departments
 Why the percentages of the budget allocated to the schools have decreased between
FY2016 and FY2025
 Differences between this budget presentation and the budgetary discussions that occurred
at the recent four-town meeting
 The makeup of the “miscellaneous” percentage of the budget—this includes pensions
 Where to find information on state aid as a percentage of the school budget and offsets
for charter schools
 Why the FY2026 projected column ends in a negative number—the numbers presented
were very preliminary numbers and will go through many updates before finalization;
discussion on this matter will not close until June 30, 2025
 Sources of the budgetary surplus, specifically which sources of revenue have historically
been underestimated to produce the surplus
 The basis of the 3% estimate for the portion of the budget dedicated to the regional
schools
Sarah Marshall expressed concern at the recurring yearly surpluses in the millions of dollars that
go into free cash instead of into vital Town and school expenditures. She also inquired about the
uses of the free cash and stabilization funds.
Irv Rhodes praised Hanneke for asking whether the Town was budgeting too conservatively.
Deb Leonard questioned why the schools were told there was no money in the budget for a
$355,000 increase when the budgetary surplus ended up being about $5 million. She emphasized
the importance of investing in schools and stated that capital building projects, while beneficial,
should not come at the expense of schoolchildren.
Bridget Hynes echoed the points made by Rhodes and Leonard, inquiring about why
“miscellaneous” made up a bigger portion of the budget than the schools. She asked the Town
Council to consider changing spending priorities to help the schools maintain their quality of
instruction, especially as state funding in constant dollars is declining.
Anna Heard expressed concern that the budget presentation did not seem to reflect the discussion
that occurred at the recent four-town meeting.
Hanneke stated that at the four-town meeting, Amherst was not in agreement with Shutesbury
and Leverett about which proposed scenario was best. She added that scenarios one and two
from that meeting would be very difficult for Amherst to meet. Steinberg echoed this point.

---

Deb Leonard expressed concern about the optics of appropriating free cash without public input
or more information on the Town’s website. She urged the Town Council to consider
approaching Chapter 70 funding similarly to how it has approached Chapter 90 funding in the
past.
Anna Heard expressed concern that underbudgeting is resulting in the cutting of vital services
and harming the Town residents who need those services. She called for more accurate
projections that could fund essential town services more adequately.
Holly Bowser explained that in FY2024, the Town brought in $4 million in additional revenue,
some of which was the result of investment and interest income. Additionally, $1.4 million of the
budgeted expenditures were not spent due to vacancies and lower-than-expected costs.
Bockelman emphasized the importance of not having a budget deficit while also acknowledging
the value of a conversation about whether the Town’s budgeting practices have been too
conservative. Steinberg described how difficult the years were when the budget had to be cut,
such as 2007 and 2008.
